🙏 Reflections

Ethical Dilemma Analysis: The 2018 MyHeritage Data Breach

MyHeritage's response to its 2018 data breach offers a useful case study in evaluating both the ethical failures that led to the incident and the professional standards demonstrated afterward. On the positive side, the company's incident response met several best-practice benchmarks: within eight hours of being notified, MyHeritage assembled an Information Security Incident Response Team, publicly disclosed the scope of the breach, and provided round-the-clock support to affected users — a level of transparency and urgency that cybersecurity commentators at the time called exemplary. The company was also honest about what had not been compromised, clarifying that more sensitive data (DNA records, family trees) sat on separate, more secure systems.

However, the underlying ethical failure is harder to excuse: the breach occurred in October 2017 but wasn't discovered until June 2018, a seven-month gap during which the exposed data sat undetected on an external server. Industry analysts pointed out that MyHeritage only learned of the breach because an outside security researcher stumbled upon it — not through its own monitoring. This raises a genuine ethical question: a company's responsibility to protect user data doesn't end with password hashing and honest disclosure after the fact; it begins with the ongoing vigilance to detect intrusions in the first place. Reactive transparency is commendable, but it doesn't fully substitute for proactive security stewardship, especially for a company entrusted with deeply personal genealogical data.

Ethical Dilemma Alternative: A Better Approach

Essential ethical principles: Responsibility/Stewardship — treating user data as something entrusted to the company, not merely stored by it; Diligence — proactive vigilance, not just honest disclosure after the fact; Transparency — clear, timely communication with those affected; Fairness — protecting all users equally, regardless of how sensitive their specific data is.

Rather than relying on an external security researcher to discover the exposed file, MyHeritage could have implemented continuous automated monitoring across all systems handling user data — including systems outside its primary production environment — paired with regular third-party security audits and a formal bug bounty program inviting ethical hackers to responsibly disclose vulnerabilities before they're exploited. This approach sticks to the principle of diligence by making security detection proactive rather than reactive, and it upholds stewardship by treating vigilance as an ongoing responsibility rather than a response triggered only after harm has already occurred.

This alternative would likely have led to significantly better outcomes for everyone involved. For users, the seven-month window during which their data sat exposed could have been reduced to days or hours, limiting the risk of the data being found and exploited by bad actors before MyHeritage even knew of the breach. For the company, earlier detection would have reduced reputational damage and potential legal liability, while a bug bounty program would have turned outside security researchers into allies rather than leaving discovery to chance. Most importantly, this approach honors the trust genealogy companies are given: people share deeply personal family and identity information expecting it to be protected proactively, not just apologized for after the fact.
