Each refinement came from asking not just whether the system works technically, but who it could affect and how — moving the proposal from a purely technical document to one that is more accountable to the people whose family history it would ultimately hold.

🙏 Reflections

Ethical Dilemma Analysis: The 2018 MyHeritage Data Breach

MyHeritage's response to its 2018 data breach offers a useful case study in evaluating both the ethical failures that led to the incident and the professional standards demonstrated afterward. On the positive side, the company's incident response met several best-practice benchmarks: within eight hours of being notified, MyHeritage assembled an Information Security Incident Response Team, publicly disclosed the scope of the breach, and provided round-the-clock support to affected users — a level of transparency and urgency that cybersecurity commentators at the time called exemplary. The company was also honest about what had not been compromised, clarifying that more sensitive data (DNA records, family trees) sat on separate, more secure systems.

However, the underlying ethical failure is harder to excuse: the breach occurred in October 2017 but wasn't discovered until June 2018, a seven-month gap during which the exposed data sat undetected on an external server. Industry analysts pointed out that MyHeritage only learned of the breach because an outside security researcher stumbled upon it — not through its own monitoring. This raises a genuine ethical question: a company's responsibility to protect user data doesn't end with password hashing and honest disclosure after the fact; it begins with the ongoing vigilance to detect intrusions in the first place. Reactive transparency is commendable, but it doesn't fully substitute for proactive security stewardship, especially for a company entrusted with deeply personal genealogical data.

Ethical Dilemma Alternative: A Better Approach

Essential ethical principles: Responsibility/Stewardship — treating user data as something entrusted to the company, not merely stored by it; Diligence — proactive vigilance, not just honest disclosure after the fact; Transparency — clear, timely communication with those affected; Fairness — protecting all users equally, regardless of how sensitive their specific data is.

Rather than relying on an external security researcher to discover the exposed file, MyHeritage could have implemented continuous automated monitoring across all systems handling user data — including systems outside its primary production environment — paired with regular third-party security audits and a formal bug bounty program inviting ethical hackers to responsibly disclose vulnerabilities before they're exploited. This approach sticks to the principle of diligence by making security detection proactive rather than reactive, and it upholds stewardship by treating vigilance as an ongoing responsibility rather than a response triggered only after harm has already occurred.

This alternative would likely have led to significantly better outcomes for everyone involved. For users, the seven-month window during which their data sat exposed could have been reduced to days or hours, limiting the risk of the data being found and exploited by bad actors before MyHeritage even knew of the breach. For the company, earlier detection would have reduced reputational damage and potential legal liability, while a bug bounty program would have turned outside security researchers into allies rather than leaving discovery to chance. Most importantly, this approach honors the trust genealogy companies are given: people share deeply personal family and identity information expecting it to be protected proactively, not just apologized for after the fact.

Ethical Dilemma Reflection: Personal View

Working through the MyHeritage data breach across this course has changed how I think about my own role in IT, not just as a case study to analyze from the outside. Before this exercise, I thought of data ethics mostly in terms of following procedures correctly — don't share passwords, double-check entries, follow the rules I'm given. This dilemma pushed me to see that ethical responsibility in IT isn't just about compliance; it's about actively anticipating harm before it happens, even when no one is explicitly asking you to.

In my own work handling genealogical records, I don't have the same scale of responsibility MyHeritage's security team had, but the same principle applies at a smaller level: I'm trusted with information that matters deeply to real people, and that trust is easy to take for granted when the work becomes routine. Going forward, if I encounter a similar situation in my career — whether that's noticing a security gap, handling consent for sensitive data, or being tempted to treat a data-handling shortcut as harmless — my approach will be to treat vigilance as part of the job itself, not an extra step. I'd rather raise a concern that turns out to be nothing than stay silent about something that turns into a breach affecting thousands of people.

This exercise helped me understand that ethical challenges in IT are rarely about clear villains making obviously bad choices. MyHeritage's team wasn't negligent in an obvious way — they responded well once they knew. The harder, more realistic lesson is that ethical failure in this field often looks like good people being insufficiently proactive, which means the standard I hold myself to has to be higher than simply avoiding obvious wrongdoing.

🔧 Usability Testing & Design Enhancements

Methodology

I conducted a self-directed usability test on the live site across desktop and mobile viewport widths, checking navigation behavior, keyboard accessibility, and screen-reader friendliness. This included clicking every navigation link, tabbing through the page using only the keyboard, and reviewing how decorative elements would be announced by assistive technology.

Issues Identified

  • Sticky header overlap: Clicking a navigation link jumped to the correct section, but the sticky header then covered the section's heading, hiding the first line of content.
  • Abrupt scrolling: Navigation jumps had no smooth transition, making the page feel disconnected when moving between sections.
  • No way to return to the top: On a long single-page site, there was no quick way to get back to the navigation after reading a later section.
  • Keyboard navigation gaps: There was no visible focus indicator on links, making it hard to tell where keyboard focus was while tabbing through the page, and no way to skip repeated navigation and jump straight to content.
  • Decorative emoji in headings: Section header emoji (🚀, 🛠️, etc.) would likely be announced verbally by screen readers, adding noise without adding meaning.

Enhancements Implemented

  • Added scroll-margin spacing to every section so jumping to it via navigation no longer hides the heading behind the sticky header.
  • Enabled smooth scrolling for all anchor-link navigation.
  • Added a "back to top" button that appears after scrolling down, for quick return to the navigation.
  • Added a visible focus outline for keyboard users and a "skip to main content" link for screen-reader and keyboard users to bypass repeated navigation.
  • Marked decorative emoji as hidden from assistive technology so screen readers announce only the meaningful heading text.

Impact

These changes directly improve the experience for two groups the original design overlooked: keyboard-only users and screen-reader users, who previously had no reliable way to navigate the page efficiently or know where they were. The sticky-header overlap was also a functional bug that affected every visitor using the navigation menu, not just an accessibility edge case — fixing it improves usability for everyone who clicks a navigation link rather than scrolling manually.
